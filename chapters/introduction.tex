\section{Overview and goals of this thesis}

It is well-known that every Boolean function $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$ can be expressed in terms of $n$ local functions $f_i\colon\F^2\to\F$, i.e., as a map
\[
x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\longmapsto (f_1(x),\dots,f_n(x)).
\]
This means that every such $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$ has an associated directed graph called its \emph{wiring diagram}, that encodes the local variable dependencies. It also motivates the terminology of calling functions of the form $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$ \emph{Boolean networks}. One of the overarching goals of this thesis is to understand structure-preserving maps between Boolean networks. That is, maps that not only preserve the structure of the global dynamics, but the local structure as well.

A dynamical system is a function $f\colon X\to X$ from a set to itself. Two dynamical systems, $f,g\colon X\to X$ are \emph{conjugate} if there is a bijection $\pi\colon X\to X$ such that $g=\pi\circ f\circ\pi^{-1}$. If $g\colon Y\to Y$ is another dynamical system and $\pi\colon X\to Y$ a surjection, then $f$ and $g$ are \emph{semiconjugate} if $\pi\circ f=g\circ\pi$. Since Boolean networks are dynamical systems on the \emph{Boolean cube} $\F^n$, all of this terminology carries over. However, as we will see, this is not strong enough to preserve the local structure as well.

Since Boolean networks for finite dynamical systems, their dynamics can be described by a finite directed graph on $\F^n$ called the \emph{state space}; edges are of the form $x\to f(x)$. If two Boolean networks are conjugate, then these states spaces are isomorphic as directed graphs. However, their wiring diagrams need not be isomorphic. In fact, an arbitrary relabeling of states need not preserve changes in a single coordinate. We therefore restrict our focus to maps $\pi\colon\F^n\to\F^n$ that preserve the Hamming distance, which we call \emph{isometries}. Algebraically, these form the automorphism group of the Boolean cube, also know as the Weyl group of type $B_n$---this is the well-known group of signed permutations, isomorphic to the wreath product $C_2\wr S_n$. It is also the  semidirect product $C_2^n\rtimes S_n$, because isometry is a composition of a coordinate permutation and coordinate complementation. Conjugating a Boolean network by one of these isometries induces an isomorphism of the wiring diagram, and thus this provides a natural notion of equivalence for Boolean networks.

Everything in the previous paragraph is well known. Our first goal in this thesis is to extend these ideas of Boolean network equivalence to reduction and morphisms of Boolean networks. A \emph{semiconjugacy} from a Boolean network $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$ to a Boolean network $g\colon\F^m\to\F^m$, with $n\geq m$, is a surjective map $h\colon\F^n\to\F^m$ satisfying $h\circ f=g\circ h$. In other words, $h$ is a directed graph homomorphism from the state space of $f$ to the state space of $g$. This defines a reasonable notion of \emph{homomorphism} between Boolean networks. However, just like how isomorphism (conjugacy) of the maps does not preserve locality, nor does homomorphism.  Our goal is to understand which Boolean network homomorphisms reduce the wiring diagram in a structure-preserving manner. To do this, we will strengthen the concept of homomorphism (semiconjugacy) and define a \emph{homometry} between Boolean networks. The following table summarizes the difference between the different types of structure preserving maps between Boolean networks:
\[
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{array}{c|cc}
& \text{preserves dynamics} & \text{also preserves wiring diagram} \\ \hline
\F^n\longhookonto\F^n & \textbf{isomorphism} & \textbf{isometry} \\
\F^n\longonto\F^m & \textbf{homomorphism} & \textbf{homometry}\\
\end{array}
\]


Initially, it should not be obvious how to properly define the concept of homometry, or how to characterize these maps. For the first point, we will say that $h\colon\F^n\to\F^m$, sending $f\mapsto g$ is a homometry if and only if it is a (weak) \emph{contraction}, i.e., that
\[
|h(x)-h(y)|\leq |x-y|,\qquad \text{for all $x,y\in\F^n$}.
\]
Then we will prove (see Theorem~\ref{thm:compatible-edge-lifting}) that an equivalent condition is that the inverse image of every edge in the wiring diagram of $g$ lifts to an edge in the wiring diagram of $f$. This leads to the concept of a \emph{node map} between the wiring diagrams. In some sense, this says that structure-preserving maps between Boolean networks are characterized by the inverse images of a key feature (variable dependencies) being preserved. This is analogous how continuous maps pull back open sets to open sets, or how group homomorphisms pull back normal subgroups to normal subgroups, or how linear maps pull back subspaces to subspaces. In all of these cases, there is a well-developed theory of these objects and their structure-preserving maps, and we will develop an analogous theory for Boolean networks. For example, we will state and prove a universal property of quotient homometries, and analogues of the isomorphism theorems, including the factorization of homometry into the composition of a quotient and embedding.

The first part of this thesis is focused on developing the theory Boolean networks and homometry, and a number of structure theorems that are analogous to those in fields such as topology, abstract algebra, and linear algebra. All of those settings have a well-established categorical foundation, leading to the categories of $\Top$, $\Grp$, and $\Vect_{\mathbb{F}}$, respectively. In the second part of this thesis, we will develop the theory of structure-preserving maps between Boolean works in a categorical framework. This will abstract the structure from the first part, and we will explore ideas such as products, coproducts, adjoints, universal properties, and so on.

\section{Basic definitions and preliminaries}\label{sec:basic-preliminaries}


The concept of a Boolean network is not difficult, but the notation and terminology can vary widely across the literature. Some authors define a Boolean network as a map $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$, whereas others define it as an $n$-tuple of functions $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$, with $f_i\colon\F^n\to\F$. The graph that describes the dynamics can be called the phase space, if one is making parallels with classical dynamical systems, or the state space, if one is viewing the network as a generalized cellular automaton. The directed graph that encodes the interactions of the variables also has many names, such as interaction graph, wiring diagram, dependency graph, coupling graph, and influence graph. Sometimes this graph is signed to distinguish activations from inhibitions, and in some applications, such as social networks, an undirected graph is used to describe the interactions. In this section, we will define the basic concepts, terminology, and notation that we will use throughout this thesis.

\subsection{Boolean states and functions}

We write $\F=\{0,1\}$ for the field with two elements, where addition and multiplication are taken modulo $2$. In particular, $1+1=0$, and $a+a=0$ for every $a\in\F$. We interpret $0$ and $1$ as the Boolean values false and true. The usual logical operations can then be written as
\[
    \Not{a}=1+a,\qquad
    a\wedge b=ab,\qquad
    a\vee b=a+b+ab.
\]
These are NOT, AND, and OR, respectively. Addition $a+b$ is exclusive OR, or XOR: its value is $1$ exactly when $a$ and $b$ are different. Thus OR and addition are different operations; for example, $1\vee1=1$, whereas $1+1=0$. Unless we explicitly say otherwise, arithmetic involving Boolean coordinates is in $\F$.

For a positive integer $n$, write $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$. The set $\F^n$ consists of all $n$-tuples of Boolean values and is called the \emph{Boolean cube}. An element $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in\F^n$ is a \emph{state}, and the coordinate indices in $[n]$ are the \emph{nodes}. There are $n$ nodes and $2^n$ states. We will usually use $x_1,\dots,x_n$ for the coordinate variables. For $2$- or $3$-node networks, we will sometimes use $x,y$ or $x,y,z$. If there is a distinguished node, like the center of a star graph, we may label it $0$ and use $x_0$ for its variable. This changes only the labels; the number of nodes will always be specified.

We write $\0=(0,\dots,0)$ and $\1=(1,\dots,1)$, and let $e_i$ be the vector whose $i$th coordinate is $1$ and whose other coordinates are $0$. Vector addition is coordinatewise, so $x+e_i$ is obtained from $x$ by changing only its $i$th coordinate. In examples and diagrams, a string such as $010$ denotes the state $(0,1,0)$.

A \emph{Boolean function} is a map $u\colon\F^n\to\F$. It can be specified by a logical expression, a polynomial expression over $\F$, or a \emph{truth table} listing its value at every state. Examples include the AND function $x_1\cdots x_n$, the OR function $x_1\vee\cdots\vee x_n$, and the parity function $x_1+\cdots+x_n$. The three-variable majority function, which we will also use, is
\begin{align*}
    \operatorname{maj}(x_1,x_2,x_3)
    &=(x_1x_2)\vee(x_1x_3)\vee(x_2x_3)\\
    &=x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3.
\end{align*}
It takes the value $1$ exactly when at least two inputs are $1$. A variable $x_i$ or its complement $\Not{x}_i$ is called a \emph{literal}.

More generally, a \emph{Boolean map} $h\colon\F^n\to\F^m$ has coordinate functions $h_a\colon\F^n\to\F$, so that
\[
    h(x)=\bigl(h_1(x),\dots,h_m(x)\bigr).
\]
A coordinate function may be constant, or it may ignore some of the input variables. We call $h$ constant if all its coordinate functions are constant.

\subsection{Boolean networks and their dynamics}

A \emph{Boolean network} on $n$ nodes is a map $f\colon\F^n\to\F^n$. Its coordinate functions $f_1,\dots,f_n$ are the \emph{local update functions}, and $f$ is the \emph{global update map}. Starting at a state $x$, the next state is
\[
    f(x)=\bigl(f_1(x),\dots,f_n(x)\bigr).
\]
All coordinates are updated simultaneously, using their values in the original state. This is called \emph{synchronous updating}, and it is the convention throughout this thesis. An AND-network or OR-network is one whose local functions are conjunctions or disjunctions, respectively, of specified input variables.

A Boolean network is a particular case of a \emph{finite dynamical system}, which is a pair $(X,f)$ consisting of a finite set $X$ and a map $f\colon X\to X$. We will sometimes write a Boolean network as $(\F^n,f)$ when we want to specify both its states and its update map. We take $n\geq1$ unless a zero-node network is explicitly introduced.

The \emph{iterates} of $f$ are defined by $f^0=\id_X$ and $f^{t+1}=f\circ f^t$ for integers $t\geq0$. Here $\id_X$ is the identity map on $X$. The \emph{forward orbit} of $x$ is the sequence
\[
    x,\ f(x),\ f^2(x),\dots.
\]
A state $x$ is a \emph{fixed point} if $f(x)=x$. More generally, it is \emph{periodic} if $f^p(x)=x$ for some positive integer $p$. The least such $p$ is its \emph{period}, and the distinct states
\[
    x,\ f(x),\dots,f^{p-1}(x)
\]
form a \emph{cycle} of length $p$. A state that is not periodic is called \emph{transient}. Since $X$ is finite, every forward orbit eventually enters a cycle. We call these cycles \emph{attractors}, including fixed points as cycles of length $1$. The \emph{basin} of an attractor is the set of states whose forward orbits eventually enter it.

A subset $S\subseteq X$ is \emph{invariant} under $f$ if $f(S)\subseteq S$. Thus a state that starts in $S$ stays in $S$ under every subsequent update. In this case, the restriction $f|_S\colon S\to S$ defines a dynamical system on $S$. We use invariant in this forward sense; equality $f(S)=S$ is not required.

\subsection{State spaces and wiring diagrams}

We will associate two directed graphs to a Boolean network. To fix our conventions, a \emph{directed graph} $G$ consists of a vertex set $V(G)$ and an edge set $E(G)\subseteq V(G)\times V(G)$. We write $u\to v$ for the edge $(u,v)$. Self-loops $u\to u$ are allowed, and there is at most one edge for each ordered pair of vertices.

The \emph{state space}, or \emph{phase space}, of $f\colon X\to X$ is the directed graph with vertex set $X$ and one edge
\[
    x\longrightarrow f(x)
\]
from each state. Every vertex therefore has exactly one outgoing edge. A fixed point gives a self-loop, and a periodic orbit gives a directed cycle. We will use state space for this graph, and also for its underlying set when the meaning is clear.

The wiring diagram describes which local functions depend on which variables. A Boolean function $u\colon\F^n\to\F$ \emph{depends essentially} on $x_i$ if there is a state $x$ such that
\[
    u(x+e_i)\ne u(x).
\]
In other words, changing only $x_i$ can change the output. If this never happens, $u$ is independent of $x_i$. Essential dependence is a property of the function, rather than of a particular expression for it: for example, $x_1+x_1+x_2=x_2$ is independent of $x_1$. We say that $u$ \emph{depends only on} a set $A\subseteq[n]$ of coordinates if any two states that agree on $A$ have the same value under $u$.

The \emph{wiring diagram} $W(f)$ has vertex set $[n]$ and edges
\[
    i\longrightarrow j
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    f_j\text{ depends essentially on }x_i.
\]
Thus an edge points from an input node to a node whose update can be affected by that input. A self-loop at $i$ means that $f_i$ depends on $x_i$. We use unsigned wiring diagrams, so the edges record dependence without assigning activation or inhibition signs.

For example, consider the two-node network
\[
    f(x_1,x_2)=(x_1x_2,\ 1+x_2).
\]
Its wiring diagram has edges $1\to1$, $2\to1$, and $2\to2$. Its state space has four vertices, with transitions
\[
    00\longrightarrow01,\qquad
    01\longrightarrow00,\qquad
    10\longrightarrow01,\qquad
    11\longrightarrow10.
\]
There is one attractor, the cycle $00\to01\to00$, and its basin is all of $\F^2$. The nodes of the wiring diagram describe the two variables, while the vertices of the state space describe their four possible joint values.

A \emph{homomorphism of directed graphs} $\phi\colon G\to H$ is a map of vertex sets such that $u\to v$ in $G$ implies $\phi(u)\to\phi(v)$ in $H$. An \emph{isomorphism} is a bijection of vertices for which this implication holds in both directions. An isomorphism from a graph to itself is an \emph{automorphism}. These definitions will let us compare state spaces and wiring diagrams separately.

\subsection{Hamming distance and coordinate faces}

To compare states, we need to count how many coordinates differ. The \emph{Hamming weight} of $x\in\F^n$ is
\[
    |x|=\bigl|\{i\in[n]:x_i=1\}\bigr|,
\]
and the \emph{Hamming distance} between $x,y\in\F^n$ is
\[
    d_H(x,y)=\bigl|\{i\in[n]:x_i\ne y_i\}\bigr|
            =|x+y|=|x-y|.
\]
These quantities are ordinary nonnegative integers. The notation $|x-y|$ in the preceding section refers to this distance. Addition and subtraction of vectors agree over $\F$, whereas counting coordinates is not done modulo $2$.

The \emph{cube graph} $Q_n$ is the undirected graph with vertex set $\F^n$, in which $x$ and $y$ are adjacent exactly when $d_H(x,y)=1$. Equivalently, $y=x+e_i$ for a unique $i$, and we call this an edge in coordinate direction $i$. The shortest path distance in $Q_n$ is $d_H$: a shortest path changes each differing coordinate exactly once. This graph records the geometry of the states, independently of the update map.

A map $h\colon\F^n\to\F^m$ is \emph{distance preserving} if $d_H(h(x),h(y))=d_H(x,y)$ for all states $x,y$. Such a map is injective and is called an \emph{isometric embedding}; when $n=m$, it is an \emph{isometry} of the Boolean cube. A map is a \emph{contraction} in our terminology if
\[
    d_H(h(x),h(y))\leq d_H(x,y)
    \qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\F^n.
\]
This is the non-strict inequality, also called nonexpansiveness. Neither condition by itself imposes any relation between network updates; that will be an additional requirement in the next chapter.

We will also use cubes whose coordinates are indexed by a finite set $A$. Write $\F^A$ for the set of assignments of a Boolean value to each element of $A$. If $A\subseteq[n]$, then
\[
    x|_A=(x_i)_{i\in A}\in\F^A
\]
denotes the restriction to those coordinates. Listing the elements of $A$ in increasing order identifies $\F^A$ with $\F^{|A|}$. The map $\pi_A\colon\F^n\to\F^A$, given by $\pi_A(x)=x|_A$, is the \emph{coordinate projection}. Similarly, we identify $\F^n\times\F^m$ with $\F^{n+m}$ by concatenating the coordinate tuples, and write a resulting state as $(x,y)$.

A \emph{coordinate face} of $\F^n$ is a subset obtained by fixing some coordinates and allowing all the others to vary freely. More precisely, for $A\subseteq[n]$ and fixed values $c_b\in\F$ for $b\notin A$, it has the form
\[
    S=\{x\in\F^n:x_b=c_b\text{ for every }b\notin A\}.
\]
The coordinates in $A$ are the \emph{free coordinates}, and the \emph{dimension} of the face is $|A|$. Restricting $\pi_A$ to $S$ gives a bijection with $\F^A$ that preserves Hamming distance. For example, $\{(u,0,v):u,v\in\F\}$ is a two-dimensional face of $\F^3$.

The whole cube is a face of dimension $n$, and a singleton is a face of dimension $0$. For this last case, we use the convention $[0]=\varnothing$ and $\F^0=\F^\varnothing=\{()\}$, the set containing the empty tuple. This notation is useful even when the networks under consideration have positive numbers of nodes. If a coordinate face of positive dimension is invariant under a network, its restricted dynamics can be written as a Boolean network on its free coordinates. In the two-node example above, the face $\{x_1=0\}$ is invariant, and its update in the free coordinate is $x_2\mapsto1+x_2$.

\subsection{State maps, fibers, and quotients}

Given dynamical systems $(X,f)$ and $(Y,g)$, a \emph{state map} is a function $h\colon X\to Y$. It \emph{intertwines} the updates if
\[
    h\circ f=g\circ h.
\]
This means that updating a state and then applying $h$ gives the same result as applying $h$ and then updating in the target system. We will sometimes omit the composition symbol and write $hf=gh$; the rightmost map is applied first. A diagram of maps \emph{commutes} when the composites along any two directed paths with the same starting and ending points agree.

A bijective state map intertwining the updates is a \emph{conjugacy}. A surjective state map intertwining the updates is a \emph{semiconjugacy}; we include surjectivity in this definition throughout the thesis. Such a map identifies the target system as a \emph{factor} of the source. For Boolean networks, these are the isomorphisms and homomorphisms discussed in the preceding section. The next chapter will impose additional conditions involving Hamming distance and the nodes.

For any map $h\colon X\to Y$, its \emph{image} is $\operatorname{im}h=\{h(x):x\in X\}$, and the \emph{fiber} over $y\in Y$ is
\[
    h^{-1}(y)=\{x\in X:h(x)=y\}.
\]
Here $h^{-1}(y)$ denotes a preimage, and does not assume that $h$ has an inverse. The nonempty fibers partition $X$. If $h$ intertwines the updates, its image is invariant under $g$, since $g(h(x))=h(f(x))$.

An \emph{equivalence relation} $E$ on $X$ is a reflexive, symmetric, and transitive relation. Its equivalence class at $x$ is $[x]_E=\{x'\in X:(x,x')\in E\}$, and the \emph{quotient set} $X/E$ is the set of these classes. The \emph{quotient map} sends $x$ to $[x]_E$. We will write $[x]$ when the relation is understood. In particular, a state map $h$ defines the \emph{fiber relation}
\[
    E_h=\{(x,x')\in X\times X:h(x)=h(x')\},
\]
whose classes are precisely the fibers of $h$ over its image.

To put dynamics on a quotient, we require $E$ to be \emph{forward invariant} under $f$, meaning that
\[
    (x,x')\in E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(f(x),f(x'))\in E.
\]
This makes the \emph{quotient update}
\[
    \bar f\colon X/E\to X/E,\qquad [x]\longmapsto[f(x)]
\]
well defined: choosing another representative of a class gives the same output class. The fiber relation of a map intertwining updates always has this property. For example, the projection $h(x_1,x_2)=x_2$ from the two-node network above intertwines $f$ with the one-node network $g(t)=1+t$. Its fibers are $\{00,10\}$ and $\{01,11\}$, and the quotient update exchanges these two classes. A quotient set need not come with Boolean coordinates or a Hamming metric; in later chapters we will give conditions that supply this additional structure.

Finally, a \emph{section}, or right inverse, of $p\colon X\to Y$ is a map $s\colon Y\to X$ with $p\circ s=\id_Y$. A left inverse of $j\colon Y\to X$ is a map $q\colon X\to Y$ with $q\circ j=\id_Y$. A \emph{retraction} onto $S\subseteq X$ is a map $r\colon X\to S$ whose restriction to $S$ is the identity. A map $e\colon X\to X$ is \emph{idempotent} if $e\circ e=e$; equivalently, it fixes every point in its image. For maps between dynamical systems, intertwining the updates is a separate requirement.
